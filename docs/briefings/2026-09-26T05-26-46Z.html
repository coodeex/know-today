👀 Keep an eye on

  • Agents that combine email, calendar, desktop control, and a dedicated inbox are pushing automation from chat responses toward operational execution.
  • Long-running coding agents can now sustain a build-and-test loop for many hours, making bounded prototype production a practical evaluation category.
  • Structured decision models that return choices, scores, or nulls could make high-volume routing cheaper and faster than prose-first language models.
  • Model procurement is shifting toward task routing, where premium reasoning is reserved for difficult engineering and lower-cost models handle routine throughput.
  • Natural-language game creation is expanding from assets toward progression, balancing, multiplayer, and full interactive experiences.

💭 Opinions worth testing

  • Opinion — Ease of onboarding is a decisive advantage for an agent because complex setup suppresses adoption before capability can matter.
  • Opinion — Visual inspection should outweigh leaderboard position when choosing models for interfaces, games, and creative coding.
  • Opinion — A premium coding model can justify its cost when it completes difficult agentic engineering work with fewer retries.
  • Opinion — Token price is an incomplete buying metric because completed-task cost also includes verbosity, retries, latency, and failure rate.
  • Opinion — Lower-cost models deserve a primary role in workloads where good-enough quality is sufficient and volume is high.
  • Opinion — Decision-first models are a meaningful category for classification, ranking, moderation, and action routing.
  • Opinion — Hands-on product impressions are useful signals of direction, but not enough evidence for a vendor commitment or production dependency.

Reflection: Measure accepted-task cost. Compare artifacts directly. Route workloads by difficulty. Test deployment friction. Separate decisions from generation.

🚀 Build something ambitious

Autonomous operating desk for a service business

Outcome: Turn inbound demand into scheduled, prepared, and continuously improved delivery without a person manually stitching together every system.

Workflow: An agent receives leads and requests, classifies urgency and fit, researches account context, drafts responses, proposes calendar slots, creates project materials, tracks delivery signals, and feeds win/loss and customer outcomes into future routing rules. It holds scoped authority for routine communication and scheduling, evaluates its own decisions against response, conversion, and delivery outcomes, and escalates exceptions involving money, contracts, sensitive data, or ambiguous commitments.

Connected capabilities: Email and calendar agents, computer-use automation for legacy portals, structured decision models for triage, premium coding agents for bespoke integrations, and a durable operational memory of account history and outcomes.

First meaningful milestone: Run a complete inbound-to-booked workflow for one service line, with every classification, proposed action, and outcome captured as reusable decision data.

Continuous product-experience factory

Outcome: Ship interactive product concepts, internal training simulations, and customer-facing demos at the pace of market learning.

Workflow: A system converts product hypotheses into playable or navigable experiences, generates core art and progression, runs automated build and test cycles, records usability evidence, produces localized explainer video, and ranks the next iteration against audience engagement. Human input is requested for brand commitments, launch decisions, and evidence that cannot be collected digitally.

Connected capabilities: Natural-language experience generation, long-running coding agents, automated tests, creative evaluation, AI video localization, and question-based content discovery.

First meaningful milestone: Produce one polished interactive concept and its localized walkthrough from a written product brief within a single operating cycle.

⚡ Fast builds to try

  • Build an inbox decision router: define five routing labels and confidence thresholds for a real message queue; get a sortable action list instead of generated replies.
  • Run a model-cost scoreboard: send the same representative tasks to a premium and an economical model; get cost per accepted result rather than cost per token.
  • Make an interruptible research console: pair voice notes with a visible agent task log; get a workflow where new instructions redirect work in progress.
  • Create a technical-video localization pack: select one existing explainer and generate searchable question prompts plus dubbed variants; get reusable distribution assets for distinct audiences.

⚠️ Caveats

  • Reported capabilities, pricing, dates, and benchmarks need independent confirmation before a purchasing or architecture decision.
  • Anecdotal demonstrations of coding, desktop control, and game generation do not establish reliability, security, ownership terms, or production readiness.
  • Computer-use and cross-application agents require explicit authority boundaries, audit trails, credential controls, and escalation conditions.
  • Model rankings judged by other models can be subjective; evaluate the artifacts and outcomes that matter in the intended workflow.
  • Access and operational suitability for structured decision tooling may be limited or uncertain.

🧠 The author's perspective

The author believes that today’s movement toward agents with email, calendar, desktop, office-suite, and coding capabilities aligns with the view that software development and company operations are becoming increasingly autonomous when a capable runtime has durable instructions, integrations, data, and appropriate authority. Long-running build-and-test loops support the idea that a harness can coordinate planning, implementation, testing, review, and deployment rather than merely answer isolated prompts, while structured decision models support data-driven routing at the operational layer. The emerging pattern of task-specific model routing further aligns with multi-agent systems in which different capabilities handle different contexts, learn from measured outcomes, retain reusable decision data, and ask people for help when real-world judgment or action is needed.
